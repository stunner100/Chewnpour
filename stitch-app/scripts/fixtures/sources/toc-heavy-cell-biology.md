# 1. Cell Structure

All living things are made of cells. Prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; their DNA sits in a region called the nucleoid. Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus enclosed by a nuclear envelope and contain organelles such as mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus. Mitochondria release energy from glucose through aerobic respiration. The rough endoplasmic reticulum is studded with ribosomes and makes proteins for export. The Golgi apparatus modifies, packages and ships those proteins in vesicles.

Plant cells also have a cell wall made of cellulose, a large central vacuole and chloroplasts, where photosynthesis takes place. Animal cells lack a cell wall, which lets them change shape.

The cytoskeleton is a network of protein fibres that holds the cell's shape and moves organelles. Microtubules form tracks for vesicles, and microfilaments let animal cells crawl and divide. Lysosomes contain digestive enzymes that break down worn-out organelles and engulfed bacteria. Ribosomes are found in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller, which is why some antibiotics can block bacterial protein synthesis without harming human cells.

# 2. Membrane Transport

The cell membrane is a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. Small non-polar molecules such as oxygen cross it by simple diffusion, moving from high to low concentration. Water crosses by osmosis through channel proteins called aquaporins. Facilitated diffusion uses carrier proteins but needs no energy. Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient and uses ATP; the sodium-potassium pump moves three sodium ions out for every two potassium ions in.

Large particles enter by endocytosis, when the membrane folds around them, and leave by exocytosis, when vesicles fuse with the membrane.

The rate of diffusion rises with a steeper concentration gradient, a higher temperature and a larger surface area. This is why the small intestine is lined with villi and why alveoli in the lungs have thin walls. A cell placed in pure water gains water by osmosis; an animal cell may burst, while a plant cell becomes turgid because its wall resists the pressure.

# 3. Cell Division

Mitosis produces two identical daughter cells for growth and repair. It runs through prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase, followed by cytokinesis. In metaphase the chromosomes line up along the equator of the cell. In anaphase the sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles by spindle fibres. Meiosis produces four genetically different gametes with half the chromosome number, which keeps the chromosome number constant across generations.

Errors in cell division can produce cells with the wrong chromosome number. Uncontrolled mitosis is a feature of cancer.
